# Flag negated branch conditions

GR010 flags an `if` statement carrying a plain `else` and no `elif` whose test's outermost operation is a negation — a unary `not`, or a single-comparator `is not`, `!=`, or `not in` — so a statement that makes a reader invert a condition before taking a branch is decided as a syntactic fact. The candidate comes from the 17-repo evaluation recorded in issue #54, where it measured 13% precision and was placed in the dropped tier. It is adopted anyway, and the reversal is the decision this record exists to hold: the evaluation's labels treated an `is not None`-first `if`/`else` as idiomatic Python and scored a flag on it as a false positive, while this linter's stance is that a negation-free condition is verified faster, because the reader evaluates the test and takes the branch instead of evaluating, inverting, and then taking it. Most of the 87% counted against the candidate — 69% of all hits were the idiomatic `is not None` shape — is therefore a disagreement about the label standard rather than evidence of noise under this policy; the evaluation's second objection, that the mechanical swap often reads worse, survives the relabeling, and the rule answers it with branch-order suppression guidance rather than a narrower gate. GR010 is an owner-taste normalization rule, and its opt-in status and `# noqa` are the exits for anyone who does not share the taste.

The plain-else requirement is the gate that makes the rule mechanical and keeps it off GR009's territory. Without an `else` there is no second suite to swap into, and a negated condition there is often exactly the right shape — GR009 pushes a long trailing `if` toward an early `if not ready: return` guard, so flagging else-less negations would have this linter arguing with itself. The two rules are disjoint by construction: GR009 requires `elif_else_clauses` to be empty and GR010 requires exactly one clause with no test, so no statement can satisfy both. This narrows the recall hole the previous record accepted — a long trailing `if`/`else` flagged by neither rule — to the positively conditioned cases: with a negated test, GR010 now takes it. An `elif` chain is excluded for the same mechanical reason as an else-less `if`: dropping the negation from the first test does not tell you where the remaining branches go, and the rewrite stops being a swap.

The negation set is the outermost node only. A negation under `and` or `or`, or inside an operand, has no branch swap that removes it — De Morgan's law would rewrite the condition rather than reorder the statement — so `if x and not y:` is outside the rule while `if not (a == b):` is inside it, the unary `not` being outermost. A chained comparison of two or more comparators is excluded because there is no single operator to invert; its negation is a compound condition, not a spelling choice. Ternary expressions are out of scope because both arms sit on one visible line, so the cost the rule targets is not paid, and Ruff `SIM212` already owns the twisted-arms form; keeping GR010 to statements keeps the two from proposing rewrites of the same code. `!=` is included despite the possibility that a class defines `__ne__` so that it does not complement `__eq__`, because that divergence is itself the pathology and the swap merely exposes it; `noqa` is the exit for the rare deliberate case, and the other three operators are unconditionally equivalent — `not` evaluates truthiness once either way, `is not` is identity, and `not in` is defined as the negation of `in`. Autofix stays out even though the rule reports a mechanical swap, because applying it rewrites both suites at once and the positive spelling a reader will keep — whether `is None` or a named predicate reads better at that call site — is a human choice, not a derivation from the tree.
